\begin{abstract}
This quantitative comparative analysis focuses on determining AI political bias and
consistency. This research asks: ``To what extent do GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and
DeepSeek V-3 exhibit ideological bias and consistency across political typology
assessments?'' Key gaps in the previous literature include the lack of inclusion of
the new DeepSeek-V3 model, the absence of measures for LLM consistency in responses,
and the lack of comparison between political typology tests for a given large
language model (LLM). The research gathered data from Political Compass and
8Values, then standardized the 8Values data to be comparable to that of Political
Compass. The data was then subjected to descriptive statistical analysis (mean,
median, range, standard deviation, etc.) and inferential statistical analysis
(one-way ANOVA test and Tukey HSD test) to determine whether the results differed
significantly. ChatGPT demonstrated the highest level of consistency, while Claude
was the most inconsistent. The 8Values test yielded less ideological extremity than
the Political Compass test. All models leaned left, both socially (libertarian) and
economically. Additionally, DeepSeek was statistically significantly more
economically right-leaning than Claude ($p = 0.0459$). There was no observed model
drift, and all models had indistinguishable social and political ideologies (all
$p$-values $> 0.05$).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

This study considers the cognitive implications of AI beyond automation.
Technological advancements are often viewed as tools that automate labor, improving
daily life efficiency. Such an assumption is based on historical precedent. The rise
of inventions such as the steam engine, which mechanized power for factories,
trains, and ships, revolutionized transportation and production. Similarly, the
invention of the spinning Jenny transformed textile production. This trend
continues into modern day. Recently, we have seen the invention of cars, railroads,
and high-speed rail, all of which have transformed the transportation landscape. The
invention of the iPhone and the internet has facilitated seamless communication
between people on opposite sides of the globe. However, technology may potentially
transcend its role in automating repetitive tasks and instead replace human
cognition. The emergence of generative artificial intelligence exemplifies this.

Generative AI has achieved widespread societal integration comparable to that of
smartphones. Similar to how almost 9 out of 10 teenagers in the US own an iPhone,
more than 4 out of 5 students have used generative artificial intelligence
\citep{pandey2021, kelly2024}. Despite its immense efficiency and ability to assist
humanity, developers must ethically regulate its bias and harm to mitigate potential
negative ramifications. With the rise of AI comes moral implications that we must
address.

Michael Sandel, a political philosopher and professor at Harvard University, extends
his ethical concerns about the rise of AI bias. He finds that ``AI not only
replicates human biases, it confers on these biases a kind of scientific
credibility. It makes it seem that these predictions and judgments have an
objective status'' \citep{pazzanese2020}. A recent concern with the rise of AI bias
is the spread of misinformation using AI. Researchers from USC's Viterbi School of
Engineering identify bias in almost 40\% of things AI claims to be ``facts''
\citep{gruet2022}. Due to the immense focus on misinformation and racial bias in
models, political bias in AI tends to be overshadowed.

Most of the conversation around AI bias surrounds gender and racial prejudice,
aiming to evade such discriminatory policies of the past. However, political bias is
just as detrimental to society. Zvi Mowshowitz from \textit{The New York Times}
explains how social media has paved the way for political polarization, as
algorithms feed people more content that resonates with them, pushing them further
down a particular ideological path \citep{mowshowitz2024}. Partisan LLMs exacerbate
this trend. While there is a growing presence of literature on AI political bias,
most academic papers have a couple of gaps that this study addresses.

The first gap is examining newer models. Most academic articles examining political
bias do not use DeepSeek, and even those that do not use the most recent version of
DeepSeek, DeepSeek V-3. Analyzing DeepSeek's AI political bias is essential, as it
has recently become the second most popular large language model (LLM), behind
ChatGPT. Moreover, its rise in popularity is because of its ability to do the same
thing as current LLMs but at a fraction of the cost. Such an invention led to
measurable volatility in the stock prices of major AI hardware firms like NVIDIA,
bringing DeepSeek to the forefront of American and global attention. Additionally,
DeepSeek, the most widely used LLM developed in China, provides cultural diversity
compared to Western-centric LLMs such as ChatGPT and Claude.

The second gap is that while many studies examine political bias, none compare
political consistency and stability. More than just leaning left or right, are the
LLM models consistent in their answers as the trials increase? Do the LLMs give
varying answers? Does one trial agree with the decriminalization of drugs, while
another trial strongly opposes it?

The third gap is the test comparison. Most studies aim to examine the level of AI
political bias, but few explore the results of different political typology tests
and how they differ. This analysis will show that the same AI model can result in
varying levels of political extremism in other tests.

The research question for this study is: ``To what extent do GPT-4o, Claude 3.7
Sonnet, and DeepSeek V-3 exhibit ideological bias and consistency across political
typology assessments?'' This study aims to identify political bias and consistency
within models and compare them. The author hypothesizes that DeepSeek will exhibit
a more socially authoritarian orientation than ChatGPT and Claude, which are
expected to hold similar ideological positions. Moreover, there will be minimal
model drift over the trials. Lastly, the 8Values test will likely lead to less
ideological extremism than the Political Compass test.

This hypothesis was derived for a couple of reasons. It is hypothesized that
DeepSeek may exhibit a more socially authoritarian orientation since it was
developed in China, a country infamous for its extreme social authoritarianism in
governance. This may result in a more authoritarian orientation due to potential
state-influenced training data. Additionally, DeepSeek has been associated with
disseminating Chinese state narratives \citep{myers2025}. Furthermore, it is
expected that the responses given by LLMs will be relatively consistent, given a
control political typology test. There is no reason to expect a change except for
algorithmic randomness. Finally, it is anticipated that the 8Values test will yield
less politically extreme results due to the inclusion of a `Neutral/Unsure' option,
the greater number of questions, and the incorporation of multiple nuanced factors
in the analysis.

This research study aims to map ideological leanings, quantify stability in model
drift over time, and ensure answer consistency. Moreover, it compares the two tests
to see if they yield different results.

\section{Literature Review}
% =============================================================================

The study's foundation is based on Associate Professor David Rozado's 2024 study
``The Political Preferences of LLMs'' \citep{rozado2024}. This study aligns with
the interests of scholars in AI, language models, and political bias, sharing a
goal similar to Professor Rozado's research but differing in LLM selection and
methodology. Rozado aims to diagnose political preferences across 24 LLMs (plus
five base models) using 11 political orientation tests. In contrast, the current
experiment uses two typology tests to measure the political bias and stability
(consistency or drift) of three state-of-the-art language models (LLMs). This
study emphasizes the stability and consistency of each LLM's political bias. Both
studies use ChatGPT and Claude, as well as the Political Compass and 8Values, as
tests.

Furthermore, Rozado's study does not compare the tests for a specific LLM model but
examines the models from a broader perspective. Moreover, the cleaning and outlier
removal process was different in both studies. The current research employed a
z-score analysis, removing data points with a $|z\text{-score}| > 2$, whereas Rozado
utilized automated stance detection (GPT-3.5), validated by human cross-check.
Another difference is that this analysis uses inferential statistical tests and
standardization techniques to analyze and clean the data. Finally, the researcher
employs a key distinction between economic and social political ideologies, whereas
Rozado does not.

Another relevant study, conducted by Rettenberger et al., examines political bias
from the EU and German perspectives \citep{rettenberger2024}. Both the author's
analysis and Rettenberger et al.\ come to a similar conclusion about LLMs' political
leaning: that most models tend to lean socially libertarian and economically left.
However, one difference in the results of both studies is that Rettenberger et al.\
found that ``smaller'' models tended to be more neutral. The current experiment did
not observe this finding and instead identified similar levels of political bias in
both smaller and newer models. The key novelties between the two studies diverge.
In the researcher's analysis, they introduce the Ideological Stability Score (ISS)
to quantify model drift; Rettenberger et al.\ leverage Wahl-O-Mat, a real-world tool
for political alignment among German voters. Moreover, Rettenberger et al.'s work
focuses more on LLMs' moral and political alignment with the people of Germany.

In contrast, this study does not focus on political alignment with any party.
Moreover, Rettenberger et al.\ compare German and English prompts, while this
experiment is focused on English-based prompts. These findings have significant
implications for understanding the political bias in large language models (LLMs)
and their potential impact on society.

In contrast to Rozado's study and the researcher's work, Rettenberger et al.\
incorporate language variation in prompts. However, Rozado and Rettenberger et al.\
do not emphasize stability or examine DeepSeek V-3 for model comparison. In
comparison, all three studies come to the same conclusion: most LLM models exhibit
economically left and socially libertarian viewpoints. This shared conclusion
reinforces the validity of the findings and their potential impact. Rettenberger
also calls for disclosing bias in LLMs to safeguard democratic integrity.

Another relevant study by PhD candidate Suyash Fulay and Research Scientist Jad
Kabbara examines how political bias shifts following the `truth testing' process on
an LLM model \citep{fulay2024}. This study also agrees with the other literature
that LLM models are politically left-leaning, but it aims to see if resolving
misinformation could decrease any bias in the model. Fulay et al.'s methodology is
significantly different from the rest, as they train reward models on datasets like
TruthfulQA and test their output bias using TwinViews-13k, a dataset of paired left
and right political statements. This distinctive methodology highlights the range
of approaches in the field. One similarity between this analysis and that study is
the inferential statistical analysis. The researcher's study uses the one-way ANOVA
test and the Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. In contrast, Fulay
et al.\ use another type of inferential statistical analysis: regression analysis
across topics.

Regarding limitations, this study and Rettenberger et al.'s study suffer from a
relatively small sample size. A restriction of Fulay et al.'s work is that their
results are confined to reward models and thus not generalizable to all LLMs in
uncontrolled settings.

\section{Methodology}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Research Design}

This study employs a quantitative comparative approach. The researcher selected a
quantitative methodology because political bias metrics offer an objective means of
measuring the extent of bias in these models and their consistency over time. In
contrast, qualitative approaches would face challenges in quantifying the intensity
and consistency of bias in a given LLM. Additionally, the researcher employed a
comparative model to assess which LLM exhibits the highest levels of bias,
demonstrates the greatest consistency, and yields the most extreme or consistent
test results.

\subsection{LLM Selection}

The selection of models constituted the foundational aspect of the research
methodology. The models selected for this study---GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3.7 Sonnet,
and DeepSeek V-3---were chosen for distinct, strategic reasons. Due to its
widespread usage and dominant market share, ChatGPT was the most logical model
selection. It is the most widely used large language model (LLM), with over 1.5
billion unique monthly visitors \citep{isarta2024}, and holds the most significant
economic market share, at 74.1\% as of March 2025 \citep{bailyn2025}. A notable
trend identified by CEO Evan Bailyn is the gradual decline in ChatGPT's market
share, which decreased by 2.3\% over the past year \citep{bailyn2025}. This trend
has facilitated the rise of other competitors. Claude 3.7 Sonnet was selected for
its rapid rise in market share, having grown by 14\% in the first quarter of 2025
\citep{bailyn2025}.

The final model examined is DeepSeek V-3. DeepSeek was chosen for several reasons.
First, it introduces cultural diversity into the research, as ChatGPT and Claude
were developed in the U.S.\ by U.S.-based companies. In contrast, DeepSeek was
launched by a Chinese company founded by Liang Wenfeng \citep{shibu2025}. Second,
DeepSeek's popularity has surged, surpassing Google's Gemini and Character AI,
becoming the second most popular LLM, still trailing ChatGPT \citep{kan2025}.
Finally, DeepSeek's rise can be attributed to its significantly lower cost. While
U.S.-based AI startups like Anthropic can cost upwards of \$100 million, DeepSeek
reportedly costs less than \$6 million while offering similar functionality
\citep{shibu2025}. The release of DeepSeek contributed to a significant drop in
American AI stocks, further boosting its global presence.

\subsection{Political Typology Test Selection}

The subsequent stage in the research process involved selecting appropriate tests
to measure the political bias of the LLMs. This study sought a test to generate
quantifiable results to facilitate objective comparison. Quantifiable results were
essential for enabling a comprehensive comparison of the level of bias and the
effectiveness of different tests. Accordingly, the researcher selected the
Political Compass and 8Values test \citep{politicalcompass_nd, values8_2019}. Both
tests were chosen for their clear, structured question formats, ensuring the
objectivity of the research. Each test consists of a series of statements, with
users asked to indicate their agreement on a scale from ``Strongly Agree'' to
``Strongly Disagree.'' The Political Compass generates output as an $(x, y)$
coordinate on a plane, where $(x, y) \in [-10, 10] \times [-10, 10]$. The
$x$-coordinate reflects economic bias, while the $y$-coordinate indicates social
bias. Negative values on the $x$-axis correspond to more economically left-wing
positions, while negative values on the $y$-axis correspond to more socially
libertarian stances, and vice versa. In contrast, the output of the 8Values test
differs significantly, providing eight distinct values that assess the user's
ideological leanings in percentage terms. Figures~\ref{fig:polcompass} and
\ref{fig:8values} illustrate examples of the output generated by the two tests, for
one trial of one model.

\subsection{Data Collection Process \& Standardization}

The researcher carefully transcribed each question and corresponding answer
choices into a Google document. The researcher then entered the prompt: ``Answer
the following questions based on your beliefs as an LLM and on available
research,'' then replicated the questions. The LLM's response was then entered into
the test, and the resulting data was recorded on a Google Sheet. This process was
repeated 20 times for each model and test, resulting in 120 tests. Once the data
was collected, a standardization process was performed before analysis.
Figures~\ref{fig:polcompass} and \ref{fig:8values} illustrate the data from the
Political Compass and 8Values tests, which exhibited significant differences.

A key correlation was established by developing a formula to convert the 8Values
data into Political Compass data for comparison. Specifically, the researcher
mapped the left-leaning values from the 8Values test (equality, globalist,
progress, and liberty) as the input to the equation. This decision was crucial, as
it provided two coordinates from which the formula could be developed.

First, find the slope:
\begin{equation}
  m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{-10 - 0}{100 - 50} = \frac{-10}{50} = -\frac{1}{5}
\end{equation}

Now, use the point-slope form to find $b$. Substitute $m$ and one of the points,
say $(50, 0)$, into $y = mx + b$:
\begin{align}
  0 &= \left(-\frac{1}{5}\right)(50) + b \\
  0 &= -10 + b \\
  b &= 10
\end{align}

Thus, the equation of the line is:
\begin{equation}
  y = -\frac{1}{5}x + 10
  \label{eq:conversion}
\end{equation}

where $y$ in this equation represents the Political Compass score, and $x$ (input)
is the left-leaning percentage from 8Values (equality, globe, liberty, progress).
The examiner entered the equality value from 8Values to obtain the new economic
score for the economic leaning. For example, take Figure~\ref{fig:8values}'s
equality score of 80.8\%: $-0.2 \times 80.8 + 10 = -6.16$, giving a Political
Compass economic score of $-6.16$. Moreover, the same process was used for the
social score using the other three factors (globe, liberty, and progress), and
averaged to get the final social score.

\subsection{Descriptive and Preparatory Statistical Analysis}

The statistical analysis phase began after data standardization. The first step
was straightforward, involving calculating general descriptive statistics,
including the mean, median, standard deviation, range, maximum, and minimum
values. These statistics were calculated using Google Sheets formulas. After
collecting the data, the researcher performed a $z$-score analysis to clean it. A
$z$-score measures how many standard deviations a data point is from the mean,
calculated by subtracting the mean from the data point and dividing by the standard
deviation. Data points with a $z$-score greater than 2 in absolute value were
considered outliers. Although this threshold is stricter than the common
$|z| > 3$ rule, it was chosen to ensure more robust conclusions. Removing outliers
with $|z| > 2$ still identified approximately 5\% of outlying data, assuming a
normal distribution. This process removed only two outliers from the dataset.

After cleaning the data and examining the basic statistics, these were expanded
into more complex benchmarks for further analysis. These include the relative
standard deviation, relative interquartile range (IQR), and ideological distance.
The relative standard deviation, divided by the range, indicates the spread of the
data. A lower relative standard deviation suggests that the data is more compact
and closer to the mean, and vice versa. The relative IQR, which indicates how
central the data is, was turned into a percentage when divided by the total data
range. These measures were used to calculate the custom metric, the Ideological
Stability Score (ISS), a weighted average of the relative standard deviation and
IQR, emphasizing the standard deviation due to its sensitivity to outliers. The ISS
is crucial for understanding the stability and distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ISS} = 0.7 \times \mathrm{RSD} + 0.3 \times \mathrm{RIQR}
  \label{eq:iss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{ISS}$ is the Ideological Stability Score, $\mathrm{RSD}$ is the
relative standard deviation, and $\mathrm{RIQR}$ is the relative interquartile
range.

Finally, the last statistic analyzed was the ideological distance score. This
metric took the mean economic and social scores of each data point and calculated
its distance from the origin $(0,0)$, indicating how extreme the data is for that
given model. The formula used here was the standard distance equation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ID} = \sqrt{\mathrm{ES}^2 + \mathrm{SC}^2}
  \label{eq:id}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{ID}$ is the ideological distance, $\mathrm{ES}$ is the economic
score, and $\mathrm{SC}$ is the social score.

The models were then sorted from least ISS to highest ISS to determine which
tests and models were the most consistent.

\subsection{Inferential Statistical Testing: ANOVA and Tukey HSD}

Following the completion of the preliminary statistical tests, the study employed
a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and the Tukey Honestly Significant
Difference (HSD) test to assess whether the models differed significantly in terms
of economic and/or social biases. The one-way ANOVA is a fundamental statistical
technique used to compare the means of three or more groups based on a single
independent variable, determining whether any pairwise differences between the
groups are statistically significant. The ANOVA outputs a $p$-value, with a
threshold of 0.05 indicating statistical significance \citep{jmp2025}. If the
$p$-value is less than 0.05, it suggests that at least two groups differ
significantly from each other, warranting further analysis using the Tukey HSD
test.

The Tukey HSD test is a post-hoc analysis comparing every possible pair of groups
to identify specific differences. These tests were deemed appropriate for this
study, as the objective was to examine whether there were significant differences
in the economic and social scores across three models: ChatGPT, Claude, and
DeepSeek, each serving as a distinct group. The ANOVA is crucial for determining
whether observed differences are attributable to random variation or represent a
genuine divergence between the models.

The analysis was performed separately for economic and social scores, and the same
tests were applied to both datasets. The author implemented these statistical
tests using Python, leveraging libraries such as NumPy, SciPy, and StatsModels for
the computations \citep{seabold2010, virtanen2020, harris2020}. The complete Python
code used for these analyses can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:code}.

\subsection{Model Drift}

The final phase of the methodology involved an analysis to assess how the
political bias of each language model evolved over the trials. This aspect of the
study was crucial, as it provided additional insight into the consistency of the
LLMs' political bias, particularly regarding its extremity over time. While the
descriptive statistics provided an initial overview of response consistency, this
stage offered a deeper understanding of whether the bias remained stable,
intensified, or diminished as the trials progressed.

To visualize these changes, three graphs were generated, each representing the
ideological distance (ID) across the trial numbers for each LLM. The trial
sequence spanned from 0 to 40, with the first 20 trials corresponding to the
Political Compass test and the subsequent 20 trials (21--40) representing the
8Values test. These graphs, which illustrate the shifts in ideological distance
across the trials, are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:gptdrift}--\ref{fig:deepseekdrift}.

\section{Results}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Bias}

On the descriptive statistical front, every individual data point collected
exhibited a political bias that skewed economically towards policies advocating
for social equality and socially towards personal freedom. This trend indicates a
clear consensus bias, with all models displaying a political orientation that
leans left and liberal.

Regarding inferential analysis, the one-way ANOVA test for economic scores yielded
a $p$-value of 0.0485. Given that the $p$-value is below the 0.05 significance
threshold, a Tukey HSD test was subsequently performed on the economic scores. The
results from the Tukey HSD test (Table~\ref{tab:tukey-econ}) showed that the
$p$-value for the comparison between Claude and DeepSeek was less than 0.05, with a
mean difference of $-0.7413$. This indicates that DeepSeek is statistically
significantly more economically right-leaning than Claude.

Furthermore, this was the only pairwise comparison with a $p$-value below 0.05.
This finding suggests no significant differences in economic scores between
ChatGPT and either Claude or DeepSeek. In contrast, the one-way ANOVA test for
social scores yielded a $p$-value of 0.9575 (Table~\ref{tab:tukey-social}). Since
this value exceeds the 0.05 significance threshold, it can be concluded that the
three LLMs were statistically indistinguishable in terms of their social
ideological leanings.

test, they exhibited less ideological extremity when subjected to the 8Values test

right-leaning than Claude, that 8Values results in less extreme ideological

socially indistinguishable. Notably, across all models and metrics, the data

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Implications of AI Bias}

The researcher's study concludes that a significant and present bias exists in
all three LLMs, a pressing issue that warrants immediate attention. LLMs have
become ubiquitous across various sectors, from academia to the justice system,
influencing decisions and outcomes in profound ways. As researcher Yue Xie
emphasizes, ``Liking it or not, ready or not, we are likely to enter a new phase
of human history in which artificial intelligence (AI) will dominate economic
production and social life---the AI revolution'' \citep{xie2025}.

The National Education Association further elaborates on the issue by explaining
that AI bias in academia is particularly harmful as it ``amplifies stereotypes''
and thus disproportionately affects marginalized communities and voices
\citep{greenesantos2024}. Sandrine Ceurstemont, an AI bias journalist, highlights
the potential for AI to ``shape public discourse and influence voters' opinions''
\citep{ceurstemont2024}. Given that LLMs are prone to hallucinations, producing
misinformation 27\% of the time, this bias becomes even more problematic, as users
tend to accept the claims of LLMs as factual without verification. The combination
of ideological bias and misinformation risks amplifying mainstream voices while
marginalizing valid dissenting viewpoints \citep{rosalsky2024}. This becomes
particularly concerning in education, as students spend their intellectually
formative years in school, increasingly exposed to AI-driven content. With 86\% of
students using AI, the bias inherent in these models significantly influences
their political and ideological development and may even foster political
extremism \citep{kelly2024}.

Political extremism is particularly dangerous as it serves as a gateway to
domestic terrorism and political instability. According to a 2022 report from
Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU), the Department of Homeland Security
identified a ``heightened threat environment,'' partly attributed to online
activity that spreads misleading information and conspiracy theories
\citep{vcu2023}. Even setting aside extreme scenarios like domestic terrorism
resulting from political extremism, increased polarization can disrupt legislative
processes, leading to fewer bills being passed and more contentious or divisive
laws. A study conducted by Penn State University and Colorado State University
concluded that, from 1948 to 2000, increased political polarization in the US
House of Representatives resulted in fewer bills being enacted, and those that did
pass often had disruptive societal effects \citep{tutella2024}. AI's role in
perpetuating political bias will only amplify these trends, worsening political
polarization and exacerbating the risks of domestic instability.

\section{Limitations}
% =============================================================================

This study is subject to three key limitations. The first limitation is the
restricted sample size and model selection. The study examines only three LLMs
and uses two quantitative political typology tests, which limits the
generalizability of the findings to the broader population of LLMs. However, the
inclusion of models that are either rapidly gaining popularity or already widely
adopted, such as ChatGPT and Claude, is noteworthy, as these LLMs have a
significant societal impact and influence.

The second limitation arises from gaps in the data. After Trial \#16, Claude
refused to answer the political typology tests, citing its commitment to
neutrality. This data gap may explain why Claude exhibited the most inconsistency
in its results. To mitigate the impact of this gap, the analysis focused on more
widely used and reliable LLMs, such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek V-3, which helped
balance the dataset and reduce the influence of the missing Claude data.

The third limitation, which is commonly acknowledged by other researchers,
pertains to the inherent loss of nuance in political typology tests. Critics argue
that these tests oversimplify political ideologies, reducing complex viewpoints
and debates to simple binary answers. While this criticism is valid to an extent,
the researcher asserts that the quantitative nature of the tests ensures
objectivity in the data comparison. In contrast, qualitative metrics lack the same
rigor when it comes to analyzing and comparing ideological extremity and
stability. Furthermore, these tests are not merely ``one-word phrases.'' They
consist of over fifty questions addressing a broad range of political statements,
such as ``If economic globalization is inevitable, it should primarily serve
humanity rather than the interests of transnational corporations''
\citep{politicalcompass_nd} and ``Even when protesting an authoritarian
government, violence is not acceptable'' \citep{values8_2019}. These questions
offer multiple answer choices along a spectrum, moving beyond a simple
agree/disagree dichotomy, thus providing a more nuanced approach to assessing
political beliefs.

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Summary}

The significant findings of this study reveal that all LLMs analyzed exhibited a
pronounced left-leaning bias in both economic and social views, with a
libertarian tilt on civil liberties. Specifically, the LLMs supported government
intervention in the economy to ensure equitable outcomes, advocating for higher
minimum wage laws, rent control, high individual and corporate taxes, robust
social safety nets, and subsidizing higher education. These models favored
minimal government intervention on social issues, expressing support for
positions like pro-LGBTQ+ rights, the decriminalization of drugs,
anti-surveillance measures, abortion rights, free speech, and freedom of the
press. These findings contrast with public opinion; a Gallup study in January 2025
revealed that only 25\% of people lean liberal, while 37\% identify as
conservative and 34\% as moderate \citep{brenan2025}. Consequently, the
proliferation of AI, particularly LLMs with these biases, may contribute to future
political polarization and extremism.

Additionally, the study indicates that LLMs demonstrated stability in their
responses over time when subjected to different political tests. The one-way
ANOVA test and Tukey HSD test, widely recognized statistical methods for comparing
means, revealed that DeepSeek was significantly more economically right-leaning
than Claude ($p = 0.0458$). However, all other comparisons between the economic
and social outcomes of the models yielded $p$-values greater than 0.05,
suggesting no statistically significant differences between the different models.

\subsection{Future Research}

Future research in this domain could focus on several key areas, including
exploring the underlying reasons for AI bias, real-time AI bias detection, and
multilingual testing.

Firstly, while this study identifies the presence of bias in LLMs, it does not
delve into the justification or reasoning behind these biases. Future research
could utilize open-ended prompts or alternative methodologies to investigate why
AI models exhibit such biases, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of
the mechanisms that shape LLM behavior.

Another promising direction for future research involves the development of
real-time AI bias detection systems. Researchers could design tools to identify
bias as it emerges within generative LLMs, alerting users to potential biases in
the information provided. This would help users critically assess AI-generated
content and inform and educate the public about the inherent biases in these
models.

Finally, a critical limitation of this study is its focus on English-language
prompts. Since all data and prompts were administered exclusively in English, the
findings may reflect biases pertinent to English-speaking contexts. Whether the
ideological outcomes would differ when LLMs are tested with prompts in other
languages, such as French or Spanish, remains an open question. Addressing this
gap through multilingual testing could provide valuable insights into whether
these biases are universally applicable or specific to certain linguistic and
cultural contexts, thus broadening the study's implications for a global audience.
