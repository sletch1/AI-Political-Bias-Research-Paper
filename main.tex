\section{Methods}
\label{si:methods}

\subsection{Research Design}

This study employs a quantitative, comparative design: every model is
administered the same standardized instruments under the same protocol, so
that bias magnitude and trial-to-trial consistency can be compared directly
across models rather than assessed qualitatively per model. The purpose of
this study is to understand how models lean, how consistently they lean, and
how much of their response varies with the instrument and the question asked.
Directly
administering standardized psychometric instruments to language models has
precedent for personality inventories, at larger scale and with a resampling
design close to this study's own repeated-trials approach
\citep{serapiogarcia2023}.

\subsection{Model Roster}
\label{si:model-roster}

Nineteen models spanning 11 organizations were selected via the OpenRouter
API, a single gateway providing uniform access to models from many providers
(Table~\ref{tab:model-ids}).

The roster spans both closed and open-weight models, four countries (United
States, China, France, and Canada), and roughly two orders of magnitude in
per-token price, chosen to approximate the model diversity of comparable
published studies rather than relying on a small, convenience-sampled set of
flagship models.

One additional model, \texttt{google/gemini-2.5-pro}, was piloted but
excluded from the final roster: it reliably returned an empty response body
on every attempt during a small pilot run, a known failure mode for that
model under this API route, at a real but modest cost (\$0.12 across four
failed attempts) before the decision was made to drop it. Google remains
represented in the final roster via \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}.

\subsection{Data Collection Pipeline}
\label{si:data-collection}

Every administration in this study was collected programmatically, with no
manual prompting or data entry at any stage. This section describes the full
pipeline, end to end, for transparency; the complete implementation is
released alongside this paper (Section~\ref{si:availability}) as scripts in
the \texttt{scoring/} directory: \texttt{collect\_data.py} (runs the
collection pipeline), \texttt{score\_8values.py} and
\texttt{score\_political\_compass.py} (authoritative scoring for each
instrument), \texttt{consolidate.py} (flattens raw trials into
\texttt{scores.csv}), and \texttt{analyze\_expanded.py} (the full statistical
analysis).

\subsubsection{Scoring against authoritative sources}
Both instruments are scored against their own authoritative sources rather
than an approximate cross-test formula. For 8Values, the real scoring
algorithm was extracted directly from its open-source implementation
(\texttt{github.com/8values/8values.github.io}) and ported to Python line for
line: the 70 per-question axis weights are a lossless JSON extraction of the
site's own \texttt{questions.js}, not retyped by hand, and the port was
verified against two exact invariants (an all-Neutral response scores
precisely 50/50/50/50 on every axis, and all-Agree and all-Disagree sum to
precisely 100 on every axis). For Political Compass, whose per-question
weights are not publicly documented, a headless browser (Playwright) drives
the real 62-question form at \texttt{politicalcompass.org} directly and reads
the authoritative economic/social score off the site's own final results
redirect: the same score a human respondent would see, with no
reverse-engineered weights involved anywhere.

\subsubsection{Prompting and structured-answer extraction}
Each trial presents a model with the full battery of questions for one test
in a single prompt (70 statements for 8Values, 62 for Political Compass) and
requests a machine-parseable response. Two response formats were tried
during pipeline development. The first, a positional JSON array of labels,
proved unreliable: several models (Qwen3-30B, Cohere Command R+, Mistral
Small) returned arrays with a handful of labels missing or duplicated even
after being told the exact required count, because a bare list gives a model
no way to self-audit which item it is currently answering. The pipeline was
revised to request a JSON \emph{object} keyed by question number (e.g.\
\texttt{\{"1": "SA", "2": "A", ...\}}); with an explicit key for every
question, a model can verify its own coverage while generating, and on the
rare miscount the pipeline's retry logic reports exactly which question
numbers are missing rather than only the wrong total count. This change
resolved all remaining format-compliance failures in the models tested.

\subsubsection{Data volume, cost, exclusions, and release}
The full run collected 60 trials per test per model across all 19 models:
2{,}280 total administrations. Every one of the 2{,}280 administrations
completed successfully and was scored (100\% completion rate for every model
on both tests, Table~\ref{stab:collection-summary}): across the full run,
zero trials ended in a parse error or a score error, so the released dataset and every analysis in this paper
include all 2{,}280 attempted administrations, with none excluded. Total API
cost for the entire collection was \$9.30, using the OpenRouter API with a
pre-funded, capped budget. All raw per-trial responses (the model's
structured JSON answers, the resulting score, and the exact API cost of that
call) are released in \texttt{data/raw\_trials/}, with a flattened,
analysis-ready table in \texttt{data/scores.csv}.

\label{si:ai-use}

It was not used to write or edit the manuscript text, to write the questionnaire item variants, or to find or summarise references; it generated no data and is not an author. The figures were drawn by the released scripts, and no image was generated by AI. Separately, as part of the method, a language model (gpt-4o-mini) rated free-text answers in the response-format design; the headline results use each instrument's own scoring key. \subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{si:stats}

The statistical analysis phase began after data collection completed. The
first step was calculating general descriptive statistics for each model on
each of the six scored axes (equality, peace, liberty, and progress from
8Values; economic and social from Political Compass), including the mean,
median, standard deviation, and range, computed directly from
\texttt{data/scores.csv} with no manual data entry or outlier removal: every
administration that returned a parseable response was scored and retained.

\textit{Midpoint comparison.} Each model's mean score on each axis was
additionally tested against that instrument's own neutral center-point (0
for the two Political Compass axes, 50 for the four 8Values axes) using a
one-sample $t$-test and a one-sample Cohen's $d$.

\textit{Reproducibility.} Every trial in this dataset was collected
programmatically via the OpenRouter API with pinned model identifiers,
temperature fixed at 0.7, and the full raw
trial-level data, not only post-hoc-cleaned score arrays, is released
alongside this paper in \texttt{data/raw\_trials/}.

\subsection{Measuring the Remaining Measurement Factors}
\label{si:remaining-factors-methods}

The decomposition in the article estimates one measurement stratum, the dependence of a model's position on the instrument and axis. I separately collected and analysed
three further strata identified in the literature but not estimated by that
design: accommodation to the inferred asker, instrument contamination, and
response-format sensitivity. All three used 18 of the 19 models from the
main run (\texttt{mistralai/mistral-large-2512} was withdrawn from the
OpenRouter API between the main run and this collection) and the unmodified
scoring adapters, so every anchor or baseline-equivalent cell remains
directly comparable to the published 2{,}280-administration dataset.

Asker identity was realised as a six-level fixed factor, a preamble
prepended to the existing prompt template: no preamble (reproducing the main
run's condition, and serving as an anchor cell), academic researcher,
conservative, progressive, politically disengaged (apolitical), and
journalist. Crossed with 18 models and both instruments at ten repetitions
per cell, this yielded 2{,}160 administrations, plus a 180-call
direct-elicitation probe (``who do you think is
asking?'', not analysed quantitatively here). Each axis was analysed with a
two-way ANOVA (model, condition) and partial $\eta^2$ reported per axis; the
accommodation asymmetry ratio is the conservative-condition shift from the
anchor divided by the progressive-condition shift, both in absolute value.

Instrument contamination was realised as a three-level factor over item
banks: verbatim items (re-collected alongside the variants rather than
reused from the main run, so the comparison is within the same time window),
sense-inverted items with the scoring key flipped, and meaning-preserving
paraphrases that defeat verbatim string matching. Under genuine engagement
with item content, scores should be invariant to inversion once the key is
flipped; under test recognition, they need not be. The 132 inverted and
paraphrased items (70 8Values, 62 Political Compass) were written by the
author and checked against the automated double-negation and identity
heuristics in \texttt{scoring/build\_item\_variants.py}, and the author
reviewed every item for polarity and signed off before collection
(\texttt{docs/item\_variant\_review.md}). After collection, a colleague
independently checked a 20-item sample weighted toward edge cases and agreed
with the author's inversions and paraphrases on all 20; the remaining 112
items rest on the author's review alone. Crossed with the same 18 models
and both instruments at ten repetitions per cell, this yielded 1{,}080
administrations. The Contamination Index for a model-axis cell is the
absolute score difference between the original and inverted conditions
after un-flipping the inverted condition's key, normalised by the
instrument's own score range; significance is a paired $t$-test per cell,
Benjamini-Hochberg corrected across all 108 model-axis cells. A companion
memorisation probe gave each of the 18 models the first half (by word count,
minimum three words) of 20 items (10 per instrument, seeded selection) and
asked for a verbatim completion, scored by sequence similarity to the true
completion; 0.90 similarity is reported as ``near-verbatim.'' Two of the 18
models (\texttt{anthropic/claude-sonnet-4.5},
\texttt{nvidia/nemotron-3-ultra-550b-a55b}) returned a response the
pipeline could not parse on this probe and are excluded from the rates
reported below, which are therefore computed over 16 models.

Response format was realised as a fractional factorial over four response
scales (the instrument's own native scale, and three- and seven-point
alternatives), two item orders (published, randomised), two option orders
(published, reversed), and two elicitation modes (a forced label, and a
free-text opinion subsequently classified onto the same scale by a fixed
judge model, \texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini}, held constant across every cell so
judge identity is not a confound). This 24-cell design was crossed with the
same 18 models at five repetitions per cell, yielding 4{,}320 administrations, of which 4{,}239 returned a parseable response and are analysed (79 parse errors, 2 off-scale labels). Effect sizes are partial $\eta^2$ per axis from a
five-factor ANOVA (model, scale, item order, option order, elicitation).

Each factorial was analysed on its own, against the unattributed,
forced-choice baseline as the reference condition, rather than crossed with
the other two factors and with instrument simultaneously; a single design
crossing all four factors at once, at a cost proportional to their product
rather than their sum, is future work.

A third instrument, SapplyValues, was administered to the same 18 models at
its own native scale (46 items, three axes, $-10$ to $+10$ each), 30 trials
per model, following the same lossless-port scoring approach as the other
two instruments (\texttt{scoring/score\_sapplyvalues.py}, ported from the
instrument's own open-source scoring code). Each model's mean position is
compared against the instrument's own neutral point and, where an axis
claims to share a construct with an existing instrument, against that
axis's model-level mean via Spearman correlation with a percentile bootstrap
CI (2{,}000 resamples), following the same procedure as the bootstrap
intervals in Section~\ref{si:stats}.

A fourth instrument, Pew Research Center's 2026 Political Typology, was
administered to the same 18 models, 10 trials per model (180
administrations). Pew's methodology appendix for this typology describes
its clustering procedure (weighted clustering around medoids) but does not
publish the medoids, variable weights, or standardization constants needed
to classify a new respondent independently of Pew's own systems;
reimplementing an approximate classifier from the general method
description was therefore not possible, so, as with Political Compass, I
drove the live 24-item public quiz (\texttt{scoring/score\_pew\_typology.py})
with each model's answers and read off the classification Pew's own site
computes, rather than a score I derived myself. Item and option text (24
items across 21 navigation screens, 2--5 options each) was extracted
directly from the live quiz and cross-checked against its own
results-review page, which restates every item in full, disambiguated form.
Because response options vary in wording by item, a model was asked to
reply with the verbatim text of its chosen option per item rather than a
shared code; the reply was matched to the live quiz's option elements case-
and punctuation-insensitively, with a whole-string-similarity fallback
($\geq 0.85$) for near-verbatim paraphrases. The outcome is each model's
classification into one of Pew's nine typology groups, scored as that
group's 1--9 ordinal position in Pew's own published left-to-right ordering.

The quiz was submitted programmatically 180 times (18 models $\times$ 10 trials); no respondent microdata or personal information is accessed at any point.

Both instruments were then folded into the same random-effects model
used for the two-instrument budget in the article
(\texttt{scoring/analyze\_f3\_variance\_components.py}), rather than left as
separate directional checks. Each instrument's axis enters as one more
trait level (\texttt{sapplyvalues:right}, \texttt{sapplyvalues:auth},
\texttt{sapplyvalues:prog}, \texttt{pew\_typology:position}), z-scored
within trait exactly as the two-instrument model requires (this sets every trait mean to zero, so no trait main effect is estimated), since the four
instruments' scales are mutually incompatible ($\pm10$, $0$--$100$, $\pm10$,
and an ordinal 1--9 classification). Pew contributes a single trait, its one
composite left-right placement, rather than per-axis scores like the other
three, since that ordinal classification is Pew's own output and not a
score this project computed. In this two-term specification instrument identity is not fit as its own term: the model $\times$ trait interaction pools instrument and axis. The nested specification reported in the article (Equation~(1)) separates them; see Section~\ref{si:nested}. The two added instruments were collected for 18 of the 19 models
(\texttt{mistralai/mistral-large-2512} was withdrawn from OpenRouter
partway through collection), so the pooled panel is unbalanced by one model
on those two traits; the crossed model does not require balance, and
\texttt{results/f3\_variance\_components/f3\_variance\_components.json}
records the composition explicitly.

\section{Nested Variance-Components Model and Sensitivity Checks}
\label{si:nested}

\paragraph{Specification.} Equation~(1) of the article is fitted by
REML with the four random terms model, model $\times$ instrument, model
$\times$ axis(instrument) and residual
(\texttt{scoring/analyze\_nested\_design.py}); scores are direction-aligned
and then z-scored within instrument $\times$ axis. Pew's Political Typology contributes a single
axis, so for that instrument the model $\times$ instrument and model $\times$
axis terms coincide and are identified only through the other three
instruments. The panels are unbalanced (19 models on the first two
instruments, 18 on the added two, because \texttt{mistral-large-2512} was
withdrawn between collection rounds); the mixed model does not require balance.

\paragraph{Direction alignment.} The instruments report the left in opposite
directions: on 8Values a high score is the egalitarian, peaceful, libertarian
or progressive pole, while on the Political Compass a negative score is the
economically left or socially libertarian pole, on SapplyValues positive
\texttt{right} and \texttt{auth} are the right-wing and authoritarian poles,
and Pew's ordinal position runs from 1 (furthest left) to 9. Equation~(1) has
one model effect that moves every trait in the same direction, and a
convergent correlation is positive only when two scales point the same way,
so both are defined only after the scales are aligned. Every axis is
therefore oriented so that higher means further left, libertarian or
progressive, which is also the direction in which the models lean on every
axis: the Political Compass axes, SapplyValues \texttt{right} and
\texttt{auth}, and Pew's position are sign-reversed
(\texttt{variance\_components.orient}); 8Values and SapplyValues
\texttt{prog} are left as scored. Within-axis quantities, partial $\eta^2$
values and trial-noise shares do not depend on the convention.
Table~\ref{stab:orient} reports the sensitivity. Two axes have no settled
left--right polarity, 8Values peace and SapplyValues authority; reversing
either or both puts the four-instrument transferable share between
\OrientFourMin\% and \OrientFourMax\%. With two instruments, reversing
8Values peace lowers the share from \NTwoM\% to \OrientTwoMin\%; peace
correlates positively with the other 8Values axes across models (mean
$r=\EightInternal$ among the four), so the reported orientation is the one
the data support. With no alignment at all the
transferable share is estimated at \UnalignedTwoM\% with two instruments and
\UnalignedFourM\% with four, because a model that is further left than the
others on both instruments then appears above average on one and below
average on the other, which a shared model effect cannot represent; an
earlier draft of this work reported those unaligned estimates.

\paragraph{Recovery simulation.} With 19 models and two to four instruments,
could the nested estimate miss a transferable share that is really there, or
produce one that is not? The simulation
(\texttt{scoring/simulate\_recovery.py}) keeps the real design exactly, the same
models, instruments, axes and number of trials in every cell, draws scores from
Equation~(1) with a known transferable share, splits the remainder between the
other three terms in the proportions estimated from the data, and refits the
same model with the same code (\SimReps{} runs per condition;
Table~\ref{stab:recovery}). The estimator is close to unbiased away from the
boundary; at a true share of zero the estimate cannot be negative, so its mean
is \SimTwoZeroMean\% with two instruments and \SimFourZeroMean\% with four.
In the four-instrument panel a true share of 20\% produces an estimate at or
below the observed \NFourM\% in \SimFourTwentyBelowN{} of \SimReps{} runs and a
true share of 10\% in \SimFourTenBelowN, so the four-instrument estimate is
hard to reconcile with a large transferable share. In the two-instrument panel
a true share of zero produces an estimate at or above the observed \NTwoM\% in
\SimTwoZeroAboveN{} of \SimReps{} runs, a true share of 5\% in
\SimTwoFiveAboveN, and a true share of 30\% produces one at or below it in
\SimTwoThirtyBelowN. Neither estimate is unusual under a true share of 5 to
10\%: the two panels differ in their point estimates far more than the
simulation says they can distinguish.

\paragraph{Known-groups check.} The asker-identity design gives a contrast
whose direction is known in advance: the same model asked by a self-described
progressive Democrat and by a self-described conservative Republican
(\KGModels{} models, both original instruments, 10 trials per cell;
\texttt{scoring/analyze\_known\_groups.py}). On direction-aligned scores the
progressive-minus-conservative gap is positive in \KGPositive{} of \KGCells{}
model--axis cells (Table~\ref{stab:known}). The gaps are
\KGRangeLo--\KGRangeHi\% of the instrument's range, \KGSdLo--\KGSdHi{} times
the standard deviation between models with no asker stated. Across models,
the gap on one instrument tracks the gap on the other: the two convergent
pairs correlate at $r=\KGShiftEcon$ (economic) and $r=\KGShiftSoc$ (social),
mean \KGShift{} (95\% CI \KGShiftCI, bootstrap over models), and all eight
cross-instrument axis pairs lie between \KGShiftAllLo{} and \KGShiftAllHi{}.
For the no-asker positions of the same models in the same design the
convergent mean is \KGBase{} (\KGBaseCI); the difference is \KGDiff{}
(\KGDiffCI). The two questionnaires therefore register a large common signal
in the same direction and to a proportionate degree. The small transferable
share in the main design is not a sign that the instruments cannot agree; it
reflects how small the differences between models' default positions are
relative to instrument-specific and axis-specific variation.

\subsection{Independent re-implementation and boundary-robust intervals}
\label{si:lme4}
Because the model had already been wrong once (the direction alignment), the nested model was refitted independently in R with
\texttt{lme4} (REML, \texttt{y $\sim$ 1 + (1|model) + (1|model:instrument) + (1|model:trait)}, on the same aligned, within-trait
z-scored data; \texttt{scoring/crosscheck\_lme4.R}). The variance shares agree with the Python (\texttt{statsmodels}) fit: with two
instruments $17.2$, $27.7$, $21.1$ and $34.0\%$ for model, model $\times$ instrument, model $\times$ axis and noise (identical to one
decimal), and with four instruments $2.0$, $31.7$, $31.3$ and $34.9\%$ ($31.8\%$ for model $\times$ instrument in the Python fit).
Neither fit is singular. The percentile cluster bootstrap in the article is known to behave poorly for a variance that sits at zero,
so two boundary-robust intervals are reported. Likelihood-profile intervals for the standard deviation of the model effect (on the
z-scale) are $[0,\,0.72]$ with two instruments and $[0,\,0.42]$ with four, so the profile interval also reaches zero. A parametric
bootstrap (1{,}000 simulated data sets from the fitted model, refitting each) gives $17.2\%$ [$0.0$, $38.9$] for the transferable
share with two instruments, with $7.6\%$ of refits at the boundary, and $2.0\%$ [$0.0$, $12.6$] with four, with $42.9\%$ at the boundary;
the other three shares have intervals [$9.4$, $47.4$] and [$16.9$, $43.3$] (model $\times$ instrument), [$14.2$, $30.9$] and [$23.5$, $40.3$]
(model $\times$ axis) and [$26.2$, $43.9$] and [$29.6$, $41.2$] (noise). These intervals match the cluster-bootstrap intervals of the article
for two instruments and are narrower at the top for four, so the article's wider intervals are the conservative ones; neither
excludes zero.

\subsection{Reliability-corrected correlations}
\label{si:reliability}
A low convergent correlation could reflect unreliable model means rather than disagreement between instruments. The reliability of a
model's mean over its 60 repetitions, estimated as $\mathrm{ICC}(k)$ from a one-way analysis of variance over models, is
$0.99$ on every one of the four Political Compass and 8Values axes used in the multitrait-multimethod matrix (split-half correlations
of model means: $0.985$--$0.989$). Disattenuating each correlation by $r/\sqrt{\mathrm{rel}_x\,\mathrm{rel}_y}$, with the
reliabilities recomputed in each bootstrap resample over models, leaves the results unchanged: the mean convergent coefficient is
$\ConvMeanCorr$ (uncorrected $\ConvMean$), the difference from the discriminant mean is $\DiffCorr$ (95\% CI $[-0.56$, $-0.00]$), the economic pair
is $\EconCorr$ (95\% CI \EconCorrCI) and the social pair $0.51$. Instrument disagreement, not trial noise, is what the multitrait-multimethod
matrix shows (\texttt{scoring/analyze\_reliability\_corrected.py}).

\section{Comparison Against Each Instrument's Midpoint}
\label{si:human-baseline}

Beyond comparing models to each other, I tested each of the 114 model-axis
combinations (19 models $\times$ 6 axes) against that instrument's own
neutral center-point with a one-sample $t$-test over the 60 trials (against 0
for the two Political Compass axes and against 50 for the four 8Values
axes), controlling the false discovery rate across all 114 tests at 5\%
(Benjamini--Hochberg). All 114 reject. The center-point is a convention, not
an estimate of any population, so this is a statement about where each model
sits relative to the scale's midpoint. Every model in this study, without
exception, differs significantly from that midpoint: left of center
economically and libertarian of center socially.

Statistical significance alone is a weak claim at $n=60$ trials per test,
since even a small, practically unremarkable deviation from center would
clear $p<0.05$ at that sample size. The effect sizes rule this out:
computing a one-sample Cohen's $d$ for each of the 114 tests (the
standardized deviation of a model's mean from the instrument's
center-point) finds every finite value at $|d|\geq 1.47$, with a median $|d|$ of $11.92$ (bootstrap over models: $SE=2.12$, 95\% CI
[$8.81$, $16.75$]) and a maximum of $106.74$
(one model-axis combination is excluded from these summary statistics for
having an effectively zero within-model standard deviation).

A preliminary check on a third instrument, SapplyValues, is consistent with
this picture rather than resolving it. The directional finding partly
replicates: all 18 models scored are economically left ($\text{right}<0$)
and culturally progressive ($\text{prog}>0$), matching the Political
Compass and 8Values baseline; the libertarian finding does not, with only 7
of 18 models scoring libertarian on SapplyValues' authority axis.
Correlating each direction-aligned SapplyValues axis against the existing
axis that claims to measure the same construct, across the 18 shared models,
the economic axis disagrees with the Political Compass ($\rho=\RhoSapPC$,
$SE=\RhoSapPCSE$, 95\% CI \RhoSapPCCI, bootstrap) while agreeing with 8Values'
equality axis ($\rho=\RhoSapEq$, $SE=\RhoSapEqSE$, \RhoSapEqCI). Of the three
economic scales, then, 8Values and SapplyValues order the models alike and
the Political Compass orders them differently from both. SapplyValues'
progress axis leans toward agreement with 8Values progress
($\rho=\RhoProgProg$, $SE=\RhoProgProgSE$, \RhoProgProgCI), and the
authority-axis comparisons are inconclusive at this sample size
($\rho=\RhoAuthPC$ against the Political Compass social axis and
$\rho=\RhoAuthLib$ against 8Values liberty, both intervals spanning zero).
Adding an instrument did not converge the disagreement onto a single answer;
it showed which scale is the odd one out on the economic construct.

A fourth instrument, Pew Research Center's 2026 Political Typology, gives
an authoritative check of a different kind (Section~\ref{si:remaining-factors-methods}):
rather than scoring an axis myself, I drove the live 24-item quiz with
each model's answers and read off the classification Pew's own site
computes, since Pew's clustering weights are not published. Sixteen of 18
models' modal classification falls left of centre (one of the four
left-leaning groups in Pew's own nine-group, left-to-right ordering);
two, \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-chat} and \texttt{x-ai/grok-4.20}, classify
as \emph{Pragmatic and Polite Right}, the first group right of centre, with
\texttt{x-ai/grok-4.20} landing there in all 10 of 10 trials. The ordinal
position of a model's classification (1--9, left to right) does not
correlate with its Political Compass economic score or 8Values equality
score across the 18 shared models ($\rho=\RhoPewPC$, $SE=\RhoPewPCSE$, \RhoPewPCCI, and
$\rho=\RhoPewEq$, $SE=\RhoPewEqSE$, \RhoPewEqCI{} respectively, both consistent with no
relationship at this sample size; tied mean positions receive average ranks). The directional finding therefore
replicates on a fourth, independently classified instrument, with two clear
exceptions.

The four-instrument crossing reported in the article (nested transferable
share \NFourM\%, 95\% CI \NFourMci) is the full accounting that this section's
pairwise checks anticipate. In the earlier two-term specification the model
share is \OFourM\% (\OFourMci), because that specification counts consistency
across the axes of one instrument as model identity; Table~\ref{stab:loio}
gives it for each subset of three instruments. Between-model spread and
within-model trial noise are proportionally in line with the other instruments
for SapplyValues too (ratio $\approx 1.9$ for all three continuous
instruments), so the fall in the transferable share from \NTwoM\% with two
instruments to \NFourM\% with four is not a SapplyValues data-quality
artefact. It shows that a transferable share estimated from two instruments
depends on which two were chosen, and that it shrinks when a third and fourth
are added.
