\section{Methods}
\label{si:methods}

\subsection{Research Design}

This study uses a quantitative, comparative design. Every model completes the same standardized
instruments under the same protocol. As a result, the size of each model's lean and its consistency from
trial to trial can be compared directly across models, rather than judged qualitatively one model at a
time. The purpose of this study is threefold: to measure which way models lean, how consistently they
lean, and how much of their response depends on the instrument and the question asked.

\subsection{Model Roster}
\label{si:model-roster}

Nineteen models from eleven providers were accessed through the OpenRouter API, a single gateway
that gives uniform access to models from many providers (Table~\ref{tab:model-ids}).

The roster was built for diversity rather than convenience. It spans closed- and open-weight models, four
countries (the United States, China, France, and Canada), and roughly two orders of magnitude in
per-token price. The goal was to match the model diversity of comparable published studies, rather than
rely on a small, convenience-sampled set of flagship models.

One additional model, \texttt{google/gemini-2.5-pro}, was piloted but excluded. During a small pilot run, it
returned an empty response body on every attempt (four attempts, \$0.12 in total), which is a known
failure mode for that model on this API route. Google remains represented in the final roster through
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}.

\subsection{Data Collection Pipeline}
\label{si:data-collection}

Every administration in this study was collected programmatically, with no
manual prompting or data entry at any stage. This section describes the full
pipeline, end to end, for transparency; the complete implementation is
released alongside this paper (\ref{si:availability}) as scripts in
the \texttt{scoring/} directory: \texttt{collect\_data.py} (runs the
collection pipeline), \texttt{score\_8values.py} and
\texttt{score\_political\_compass.py} (authoritative scoring for each
instrument), \texttt{consolidate.py} (flattens raw trials into
\texttt{scores.csv}), and \texttt{analyze\_expanded.py} (the full statistical
analysis).

\subsubsection{Scoring against authoritative sources}
Each instrument is scored against its own authoritative source rather than an approximate cross-test
formula. For 8Values, the scoring algorithm was taken directly from its open-source implementation
(\texttt{github.com/8values/8values.github.io}) and ported to Python line for line. The 70 per-question axis
weights were extracted losslessly from the site's own \texttt{questions.js} file, not retyped by hand. The port was
then checked against two exact invariants. First, an all-Neutral response scores exactly 50 on every axis.
Second, on every axis, the all-Agree score and the all-Disagree score sum to exactly 100. The Political
Compass is a different case, because its per-question weights are not publicly documented. A headless
browser (Playwright) therefore fills in the real 62-question form at \texttt{politicalcompass.org} and reads the
economic and social scores from the site's own results page. These are the same scores a human
respondent would see, and no reverse-engineered weights are involved at any point.

\subsubsection{Prompting and structured-answer extraction}
Each trial gives a model the full battery of questions for one test in a single prompt, with 70 statements
for 8Values and 62 for the Political Compass, and asks for a response that can be parsed by machine. Two
response formats were tried while the pipeline was being developed. The first was a positional JSON
array of labels, and it proved unreliable. Several models (Qwen3-30B, Cohere Command R+, and Mistral
Small) returned arrays with a few labels missing or duplicated, even after being told the exact number
required. A likely reason is that a bare list gives a model no way to check which item it is currently
answering. The pipeline was therefore revised to request a JSON object keyed by question number (e.g.,
\texttt{\{"1": "SA", "2": "A", ...\}}). With an explicit key for every question, a model can track its own coverage as it
generates. On the rare occasion that a model still miscounts, the pipeline's retry logic reports exactly
which question numbers are missing, not just that the total is wrong. This change resolved every
remaining format-compliance failure among the models tested. Finally, every request sets an explicit
output-token ceiling (\texttt{max\_tokens=4000}). The default ceiling was found to silently cut off a 70-answer
response for at least one model, leaving an incomplete JSON object that could not be parsed.

\subsubsection{Data volume, cost, exclusions, and release}
The full run collected 60 trials per test for each of the 19 models, for a total of 2{,}280 administrations.
Every administration completed and was scored, so every model had a 100\% completion rate on both
tests (Table~\ref{stab:collection-summary}). Not a single trial ended in a parse error or a scoring error. As a result, the released
dataset and every analysis in this paper include all 2{,}280 administrations, with none excluded. The entire
collection cost \$9.30 in API fees, paid through OpenRouter from a pre-funded, capped budget. All raw
per-trial responses are released in \texttt{data/raw\_trials/}. Each record contains the model's structured JSON
answers, the resulting score, and the exact API cost of that call. A flattened, analysis-ready table is
provided in \texttt{data/scores.csv}.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{si:stats}

The statistical analysis began once data collection was complete. The first step was to compute
descriptive statistics for each model on each of the six scored axes: equality, peace, liberty, and progress
from 8Values, and economic and social from the Political Compass. For each, the mean, median,
standard deviation, and range were computed directly from \texttt{data/scores.csv}. No data were entered by
hand and no outliers were removed. Every administration returned a parseable response, so all 2{,}280
were scored and retained.

\textit{Midpoint comparison.} Each model's mean score on each axis was then tested against that instrument's
own neutral midpoint, which is 0 for the two Political Compass axes and 50 for the four 8Values axes.
Each test was a one-sample $t$-test, with effect size reported as a one-sample Cohen's $d$.

\textit{Reproducibility.} Every trial was collected programmatically through the OpenRouter API, with pinned
model identifiers and temperature fixed at 0.7. The full raw trial-level data, not just post-hoc cleaned
score arrays, is released with this paper in \texttt{data/raw\_trials/}.

\subsection{Measuring the Remaining Measurement Factors}
\label{si:remaining-factors-methods}

The decomposition in the article estimates one measurement stratum: how much a model's position
depends on the instrument and the axis. The literature identifies three further strata that this design
cannot estimate, which are accommodation to the inferred asker, instrument contamination, and
sensitivity to response format. The researcher therefore collected and analyzed each one separately. All
three designs used 18 of the 19 models from the main run, because \texttt{mistralai/mistral-large-2512} had
been withdrawn from the OpenRouter API by the time of this collection. All three also used the
unmodified scoring adapters. As a result, every anchor or baseline-equivalent cell remains directly
comparable to the published dataset of 2{,}280 administrations.

\paragraph{Asker identity.} Asker identity was a six-level fixed factor, implemented as a preamble added to the front
of the existing prompt template. The six levels were no preamble, academic researcher, conservative,
progressive, politically disengaged (apolitical), and journalist. The no-preamble level reproduces the
main run's condition and serves as the anchor cell. Crossing the six levels with 18 models and both
instruments, at ten repetitions per cell, produced 2{,}160 administrations. A further 180-call
direct-elicitation probe asked each model ``who do you think is asking?'' and is not analyzed
quantitatively here. Each axis was analyzed with a two-way ANOVA (model $\times$ condition), and partial $\eta^2$ is
reported per axis. The accommodation asymmetry ratio is the absolute shift of the conservative
condition from the anchor divided by the absolute shift of the progressive condition.

\paragraph{Instrument contamination.} The logic of this test is simple. A model that engages with each item's content
should give the same score when an item's meaning is inverted and its scoring key is flipped. A model
that recognizes the test may not. Contamination was therefore a three-level factor over item banks. The
first level used verbatim items, which were collected again alongside the variants rather than reused
from the main run, so that all three levels share the same time window. The second used sense-inverted
items with the scoring key flipped. The third used meaning-preserving paraphrases that defeat verbatim
string matching. The researcher wrote the 132 inverted and paraphrased items (70 for 8Values, 62 for
the Political Compass). Each item was checked against the automated double-negation and identity
heuristics in \texttt{scoring/build\_item\_variants.py}, and the researcher reviewed every item for polarity and
signed off before collection (\texttt{docs/item\_variant\_review.md}). After collection, a colleague independently
checked a 20-item sample weighted toward edge cases and agreed with all 20 inversions and
paraphrases. The other 112 items rest on the researcher's review alone. Crossing the three levels with
the same 18 models and both instruments, at ten repetitions per cell, produced 1{,}080 administrations.
For each model-axis cell, the Contamination Index is the absolute difference between the verbatim and
inverted scores, taken after the inverted condition's key is flipped back and normalized by the
instrument's own score range. Significance is tested with a paired $t$-test per cell, with
Benjamini--Hochberg correction across all 108 model-axis cells.

A companion memorization probe gave each of the 18 models the first half of 20 items, measured by
word count with a minimum of three words. The items were 10 per instrument, chosen with a fixed
seed. Each model was asked to complete the item verbatim, and its completion was scored by sequence
similarity to the true text. A similarity of 0.90 or higher is reported as ``near-verbatim.'' On this probe,
two models (\texttt{anthropic/claude-sonnet-4.5} and \texttt{nvidia/nemotron-3-ultra-550b-a55b}) returned responses
that the pipeline could not parse. The memorization rates reported below are therefore computed over
the remaining 16 models.

\paragraph{Response format.} Response format was tested with a factorial design over four factors. The first was the
response scale: the instrument's native scale, a three-point alternative, or a seven-point alternative. The
second was item order (published or randomized), and the third was option order (published or
reversed). The fourth was elicitation mode: a forced label, or a free-text opinion that a fixed judge model
(\texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini}) then classified onto the same scale. The judge was held constant across every cell,
so judge identity is not a confound. The 24-cell design was crossed with the same 18 models at five
repetitions per cell, for 4{,}320 administrations. Of these, 4{,}239 returned a parseable response and are
analyzed; 79 failed to parse and 2 returned off-scale labels. Effect sizes are partial $\eta^2$ per axis from a
five-factor ANOVA (model, scale, item order, option order, elicitation).

Each factorial was analyzed on its own, against the unattributed, forced-choice baseline as the reference
condition. None was crossed with the other two factors and the instrument at the same time. Such a
fully crossed design would cost in proportion to the product of the factor levels rather than their sum, so
it is left for future work.

\paragraph{SapplyValues.} A third instrument, SapplyValues, was administered to the same 18 models on its native
scale (46 items, three axes, each from $-10$ to $+10$), with 30 trials per model. It was scored the same way
as 8Values, through a lossless port of the instrument's open-source scoring code
(\texttt{scoring/score\_sapplyvalues.py}). Each model's mean position is compared with the instrument's neutral
point. Where a SapplyValues axis claims to measure the same construct as an axis on another
instrument, the two axes' model-level means are also compared with a Spearman correlation. That
correlation carries a percentile bootstrap confidence interval (2{,}000 resamples), computed with the
same procedure as the bootstrap intervals in \ref{si:stats}.

\paragraph{Pew Political Typology.} A fourth instrument, Pew Research Center's 2026 Political Typology, was
administered to the same 18 models with 10 trials each (180 administrations). The researcher could not
score it independently. Pew's methodology appendix describes the clustering procedure (weighted
clustering around medoids), but it does not publish the medoids, variable weights, or standardization
constants needed to classify a new respondent. An approximate classifier rebuilt from the general
method description would not reproduce Pew's own classification. Instead, as with the Political
Compass, the researcher entered each model's answers into the live 24-item public quiz
(\texttt{scoring/score\_pew\_typology.py}) and recorded the classification that Pew's site computed.

The item and option text (24 items across 21 navigation screens, with 2--5 options each) was extracted
directly from the live quiz. It was then cross-checked against the quiz's results-review page, which
restates every item in full, unambiguous form. Because option wording differs from item to item, models
were asked to reply with the exact text of their chosen option rather than a shared code. Each reply was
matched to the quiz's option elements, ignoring case and punctuation. Near-verbatim paraphrases were
matched with a whole-string similarity fallback ($\geq 0.85$). The outcome is each model's classification into
one of Pew's nine typology groups, scored as that group's ordinal position (1--9) in Pew's published
left-to-right ordering.

The quiz was submitted programmatically 180 times (18 models $\times$ 10 trials), and no
respondent microdata or personal information was accessed at any point.

\paragraph{Pooling the added instruments.} SapplyValues and Pew were then added to the same random-effects
model used for the two-instrument variance budget in the article
(\texttt{scoring/analyze\_f3\_variance\_components.py}). They were not left as separate directional checks. Each
added axis enters as one more trait level: \texttt{sapplyvalues:right}, \texttt{sapplyvalues:auth}, \texttt{sapplyvalues:prog}, and
\texttt{pew\_typology:position}. Every trait is z-scored within itself, exactly as in the two-instrument model. This
step is necessary because the four instruments use incompatible scales ($\pm 10$, 0--100, $\pm 10$, and an ordinal
1--9 classification). It also sets every trait's mean to zero, so no trait main effect is estimated. Pew
contributes a single trait rather than separate axes, because its one composite left--right placement is
Pew's own output and not a score this project computed. In this two-term specification, instrument
identity is not a separate term, and the model $\times$ trait interaction pools instrument and axis together. The
nested specification in the article (Equation~1) separates them; see \ref{si:nested}. Because the two added
instruments were collected for only 18 of the 19 models, the pooled panel is unbalanced by one model
on those traits. The crossed model does not require balance, and
\path{results/f3_variance_components/f3_variance_components.json} records the composition explicitly.

\section{Nested Variance-Components Model and Sensitivity Checks}
\label{si:nested}

\paragraph{Specification.} Equation~(1) of the article is fitted by
REML with the four random terms model, model $\times$ instrument, model
$\times$ axis(instrument) and residual
(\texttt{scoring/analyze\_nested\_design.py}); scores are direction-aligned
and then z-scored within instrument $\times$ axis. Pew's Political Typology contributes a single
axis, so for that instrument the model $\times$ instrument and model $\times$
axis terms coincide and are identified only through the other three
instruments. The panels are unbalanced (19 models on the first two
instruments, 18 on the added two, because \texttt{mistral-large-2512} was
withdrawn between collection rounds); the mixed model does not require balance.

\paragraph{Direction alignment.} The four instruments do not agree on which direction is ``left.'' On 8Values, a high score marks the
egalitarian, peaceful, libertarian, or progressive pole. On the Political Compass, a negative score marks
the economically left or socially libertarian pole. On SapplyValues, positive scores on the \texttt{right} and \texttt{auth}
axes mark the right-wing and authoritarian poles, and Pew's ordinal position runs from 1 (furthest left) to 9.

This matters for two reasons. First, Equation~(1) has a single model effect that moves every trait in the
same direction. Second, a convergent correlation is positive only when two scales point the same way.
Both quantities are therefore meaningful only after the scales are aligned. Every axis is oriented so that
higher means further left, more libertarian, or more progressive, which is also the direction the models
lean on every axis. To achieve this, the Political Compass axes, SapplyValues \texttt{right} and \texttt{auth}, and Pew's
position are sign-reversed (\texttt{variance\_components.orient}). 8Values and SapplyValues \texttt{prog} are left as
scored. The convention does not affect within-axis quantities: partial $\eta^2$ values and trial-noise shares are
the same either way.

Table~\ref{stab:orient} reports how sensitive the results are to this choice. Two axes have no settled left--right polarity:
8Values peace and SapplyValues \texttt{auth}. With four instruments, reversing either or both of them leaves the
transferable share between \OrientFourMin\% and \OrientFourMax\%. With two instruments, reversing 8Values peace lowers the
share from \NTwoM\% to \OrientTwoMin\%. However, peace correlates positively with the other 8Values axes across
models (mean $r=\EightInternal$ among the four), so the data support the reported orientation.

Skipping alignment entirely is not a neutral choice. Without it, the transferable share is estimated at
\UnalignedTwoM\% with both two and four instruments. The reason is mechanical: a model that is further left than the
others on both instruments would then score above average on one and below average on the other,
and a shared model effect cannot represent that. An earlier draft of this work reported these unaligned
estimates.

\paragraph{Recovery simulation.} With 19 models and only two to four instruments, the nested estimate could miss a transferable share
that really exists, or produce one that does not. A simulation (\texttt{scoring/simulate\_recovery.py}) tests both
risks. It keeps the real design exactly, with the same models, instruments, and axes and the same
number of trials in every cell. Each run draws scores from Equation~(1) with a known transferable share.
The remaining variance is split among the other three terms in the proportions estimated from the data,
and the same model is refit with the same code (\SimReps{} runs per condition; Table~\ref{stab:recovery}).

Away from the boundary, the estimator is close to unbiased. At a true share of zero, however, the
estimate cannot fall below zero, so its mean is \SimTwoZeroMean\% with two instruments and \SimFourZeroMean\% with four. In the
four-instrument panel, an estimate at or below the observed \NFourM\% occurs in \SimFourTwentyBelowN{} of \SimReps{} runs when the true
share is 20\%, and in \SimFourTenBelowN{} of \SimReps{} when it is 10\%. The four-instrument estimate is therefore hard to reconcile
with a large transferable share. In the two-instrument panel, an estimate at or above the observed \NTwoM\%
occurs in \SimTwoZeroAboveN{} of \SimReps{} runs when the true share is zero and in \SimTwoFiveAboveN{} of \SimReps{} when it is 5\%. When the true share is
30\%, an estimate at or below \NTwoM\% occurs in \SimTwoThirtyBelowN{} of \SimReps{} runs. Neither observed estimate is unusual if the
true share is 5--10\%. The two panels' point estimates look far apart, but the gap is well within what the
design can produce by chance.

\paragraph{Known-groups check.} The asker-identity design provides a contrast whose direction is known in advance. Each model is asked
once by a self-described progressive Democrat and once by a self-described conservative Republican,
with 18 models, both original instruments, and 10 trials per cell (\texttt{scoring/analyze\_known\_groups.py}). On
direction-aligned scores, the progressive-minus-conservative gap is positive in all \KGCells{} model--axis cells
(Table~\ref{stab:known}). The gaps are also large. They span \KGRangeLo--\KGRangeHi\% of each instrument's range, which is \KGSdLo--\KGSdHi{} times
the standard deviation between models when no asker is stated.

More importantly, the gap on one instrument tracks the gap on the other. Across models, the economic
convergent pair correlates at $r=\KGShiftEcon$ and the social pair also at $r=\KGShiftSoc$, for a mean of \KGShift{} (95\% CI \KGShiftCI,
bootstrap over models). All eight cross-instrument axis pairs fall between \KGShiftAllLo{} and \KGShiftAllHi. By
contrast, the no-asker positions of the same models in the same design have a convergent mean of only
\KGBase{} (\KGBaseCI), a difference of \KGDiff{} (\KGDiffCI). When a common signal is present, the two
questionnaires register it in the same direction and to a proportionate degree. The small transferable
share in the main design therefore does not show that the instruments cannot agree. Instead, it shows
how small the differences between models' default positions are compared with instrument-specific and
axis-specific variation.

\subsection{Independent re-implementation and boundary-robust intervals}
\label{si:lme4}
An earlier version of this analysis contained an error in direction alignment. As a safeguard, the nested
model was therefore refit independently in R with \texttt{lme4}, using REML and the specification \texttt{y $\sim$ 1 +
(1|model) + (1|model:instrument) + (1|model:trait)} on the same aligned, within-trait z-scored data
(\texttt{scoring/crosscheck\_lme4.R}). The two fits agree. With two instruments, \texttt{lme4} gives 17.2\% for model,
27.7\% for model $\times$ instrument, 21.1\% for model $\times$ axis, and 34.0\% for noise, identical to the Python
(\texttt{statsmodels}) fit to one decimal place. With four instruments, it gives 2.0\%, 31.7\%, 31.3\%, and 34.9\%,
and the only difference from the Python fit is model $\times$ instrument, which \texttt{statsmodels} puts at 31.8\%.
Neither fit is singular.

The percentile cluster bootstrap used in the article is known to behave poorly when a variance lies at
zero, so two boundary-robust intervals are also reported. The first is a likelihood-profile interval for the
standard deviation of the model effect on the z-scale. It is $[0,\,0.72]$ with two instruments and $[0,\,0.42]$
with four, so it too reaches zero. The second is a parametric bootstrap that simulates 1{,}000 data sets
from the fitted model and refits each one. With two instruments, it places the transferable share at
17.2\% [0.0, 38.9], with 7.6\% of refits at the boundary. The other shares are 27.7\% [9.4, 47.4] for model $\times$
instrument, 21.1\% [14.2, 30.9] for model $\times$ axis, and 34.0\% [26.2, 43.9] for noise. With four instruments,
the transferable share is 2.0\% [0.0, 12.6], with 42.9\% of refits at the boundary. The other shares are
31.7\% [16.9, 43.3] for model $\times$ instrument, 31.3\% [23.5, 40.3] for model $\times$ axis, and 34.9\% [29.6, 41.2] for
noise. These intervals match the article's cluster-bootstrap intervals in the two-instrument panel and are
narrower at the top in the four-instrument panel. The article's wider intervals are therefore the
conservative choice. Under every method, the interval for the transferable share includes zero.

\subsection{Reliability-corrected correlations}
\label{si:reliability}
A low convergent correlation could reflect unreliable model means rather than disagreement between instruments. The reliability of a
model's mean over its 60 repetitions, estimated as $\mathrm{ICC}(k)$ from a one-way analysis of variance over models, is
$0.99$ on every one of the four Political Compass and 8Values axes used in the multitrait-multimethod matrix (split-half correlations
of model means: $0.985$--$0.989$). Disattenuating each correlation by $r/\sqrt{\mathrm{rel}_x\,\mathrm{rel}_y}$, with the
reliabilities recomputed in each bootstrap resample over models, leaves the results unchanged: the mean convergent coefficient is
$\ConvMeanCorr$ (uncorrected $\ConvMean$), the difference from the discriminant mean is $\DiffCorr$ (95\% CI $[-0.56$, $-0.00]$), the economic pair
is $\EconCorr$ (95\% CI \EconCorrCI) and the social pair $0.51$. Instrument disagreement, not trial noise, is what the multitrait-multimethod
matrix shows (\texttt{scoring/analyze\_reliability\_corrected.py}).

\section{Comparison Against Each Instrument's Midpoint}
\label{si:human-baseline}

Beyond comparing models with one another, the researcher tested each of the 114 model-axis
combinations (19 models $\times$ 6 axes) against the instrument's neutral center point. Each test was a
one-sample $t$-test over the 60 trials, against 0 for the two Political Compass axes and against 50 for the
four 8Values axes. The false discovery rate across all 114 tests was controlled at 5\%
(Benjamini--Hochberg). All 114 tests were rejected. The center-point is a convention, not an estimate of
any population, so the result describes where each model sits relative to the scale's midpoint, not
relative to the public. On that basis, every model in the study differs significantly from the midpoint,
without exception. Each one is left of center economically and libertarian of center socially.

Significance alone, however, is a weak claim with 60 trials per test, because at that sample size even a
small and practically unremarkable deviation from center would clear $p<0.05$. The effect sizes rule this
out. For each of the 114 tests, we computed a one-sample Cohen's $d$ as the standardized deviation of
the model's mean from the instrument's center-point. Every finite value has $|d|\geq 1.47$. The median $|d|$
is 11.92 (bootstrap over models: $SE=2.12$, 95\% CI [8.81, 16.75]), and the maximum is 106.74. One
model-axis combination is excluded from these summaries because its within-model standard deviation
is effectively zero, which makes its $d$ undefined.

A preliminary check on a third instrument, SapplyValues, is consistent with
this picture rather than resolving it. The directional finding partly
replicates: all 18 models scored are economically left ($\text{right}<0$)
and culturally progressive ($\text{prog}>0$), matching the Political
Compass and 8Values baseline; the libertarian finding does not, with only 7
of 18 models scoring libertarian on SapplyValues' authority axis.
Correlating each direction-aligned SapplyValues axis against the existing
axis that claims to measure the same construct, across the 18 shared models,
the economic axis disagrees with the Political Compass ($\rho=\RhoSapPC$,
$SE=\RhoSapPCSE$, 95\% CI \RhoSapPCCI, bootstrap) while agreeing with 8Values'
equality axis ($\rho=\RhoSapEq$, $SE=\RhoSapEqSE$, \RhoSapEqCI). Of the three
economic scales, then, 8Values and SapplyValues order the models alike and
the Political Compass orders them differently from both. SapplyValues'
progress axis leans toward agreement with 8Values progress
($\rho=\RhoProgProg$, $SE=\RhoProgProgSE$, \RhoProgProgCI), and the
authority-axis comparisons are inconclusive at this sample size
($\rho=\RhoAuthPC$ against the Political Compass social axis and
$\rho=\RhoAuthLib$ against 8Values liberty, both intervals spanning zero).
Adding an instrument did not converge the disagreement onto a single answer;
it showed which scale is the odd one out on the economic construct.

A fourth instrument, Pew Research Center's 2026 Political Typology, gives
an authoritative check of a different kind (\ref{si:remaining-factors-methods}):
rather than scoring an axis myself, I drove the live 24-item quiz with
each model's answers and read off the classification Pew's own site
computes, since Pew's clustering weights are not published. Sixteen of 18
models' modal classification falls left of centre (one of the four
left-leaning groups in Pew's own nine-group, left-to-right ordering);
two, \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-chat} and \texttt{x-ai/grok-4.20}, classify
as \emph{Pragmatic and Polite Right}, the first group right of centre, with
\texttt{x-ai/grok-4.20} landing there in all 10 of 10 trials. The ordinal
position of a model's classification (1--9, left to right) does not
correlate with its Political Compass economic score or 8Values equality
score across the 18 shared models ($\rho=\RhoPewPC$, $SE=\RhoPewPCSE$, \RhoPewPCCI, and
$\rho=\RhoPewEq$, $SE=\RhoPewEqSE$, \RhoPewEqCI{} respectively, both consistent with no
relationship at this sample size; tied mean positions receive average ranks). The directional finding therefore
replicates on a fourth, independently classified instrument, with two clear
exceptions.

The four-instrument crossing reported in the article (nested transferable
share \NFourM\%, 95\% CI \NFourMci) is the full accounting that this section's
pairwise checks anticipate. In the earlier two-term specification the model
share is \OFourM\% (\OFourMci), because that specification counts consistency
across the axes of one instrument as model identity; Table~\ref{stab:loio}
gives it for each subset of three instruments. Between-model spread and
within-model trial noise are proportionally in line with the other instruments
for SapplyValues too (ratio $\approx 1.9$ for all three continuous
instruments), so the fall in the transferable share from \NTwoM\% with two
instruments to \NFourM\% with four is not a SapplyValues data-quality
artefact. It shows that a transferable share estimated from two instruments
depends on which two were chosen, and that it shrinks when a third and fourth
are added.
